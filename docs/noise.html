1. Visualizing sound

A microphone turns air wiggles into a long list of numbers, thousands every second. Each picture in the app is a different way of looking at that list.

The waveform is the wiggle itself, like a seismograph. The meter is how big the wiggles are right now. The spectrum sorts the sound by pitch, low notes on the left and high on the right, like a piano. The spectrogram is the spectrum drawn over and over as time moves, so you can see a hum as a straight line and your voice as wavy stripes.

Loudness is measured in dB going down from zero. Zero is the loudest the mic can record; -20 is quieter; -60 is very quiet. Every 6 dB lower is half as big a wiggle.

The mic delivers samples: numbers between -1 and +1, 48,000 a second on a Mac, 16,000 in many speech systems. Every reading in the app is in dBFS, decibels relative to full scale: 0 dBFS is the largest value the converter can represent, and everything real is negative. 20 dB is a factor of 10 in amplitude; 6 dB is a factor of 2.

Waveform

Samples against time. Audio Debugger scales it to its own peak so a quiet mic still fills the panel, which means its height says nothing about level: read the ± scale in its corner, or the meter.

RMS and peak

Peak is the largest sample in a window; it tells you how close you are to clipping. RMS (root mean square) averages the energy over the window; it tracks loudness. Speech peaks sit 12 to 20 dB above its RMS, so a voice at -30 RMS can peak near -12. The meter shows both (RMS green, peak blue, the held peak red) from -60 to 0, with guides at -18 (a common speech target) and -6 (getting hot). Anything at or past full scale is counted as clipped: that damage cannot be undone downstream.

A-weighting vs flat

Sound level meters often report dB(A), which turns low and very high frequencies down to roughly match how hearing works at moderate levels: about -19 dB at 100 Hz and -30 dB at 50 Hz, nothing at 1 kHz. Audio Debugger is flat: every frequency counts the same. So a fan's rumble or 60 Hz hum you barely hear can lift the flat noise floor by several dB. If the floor looks worse than the room sounds, check the left end of the spectrum.

Spectrum

An FFT of the newest 4096 samples: level per frequency, on a log frequency axis (20 Hz to half the sample rate) so each octave gets equal width. The dashed amber line is a slowly decaying peak hold, which shows where energy has been, not only where it is now.

Spectrogram

Spectra stacked over time: time runs left to right, frequency (0 to 8 kHz) bottom to top, brightness is level. It is the most useful single picture for noise, because different sources have different shapes:

Steady sources (hum, fans) are the easy kind: they are always there, so the gaps between words measure them, and noise reduction can learn and subtract them. Moving and impulsive sources (other voices, clanks) are hard for both.

2. Where the voice sits, and how loud the room is

Your voice has a low part and a high part. The low part is the hum of your vocal cords, the note you sing. The high part is the hiss of letters like s, f and t, and it is what lets you tell "sip" from "ship". Speech recognizers need both.

To know if a room is too noisy, listen to it in two places: the quiet moments between words (that is the noise floor, the room's own sound) and the loud moments when you talk (the speech level). The bigger the gap between them, the easier it is to understand you. A gap of 30 dB or more is great. Under 15 is hard work for a computer.

At the gym with a mic right by his mouth, Igor's quiet moments sat at -62 and his voice at -17: a 45 dB gap. The gym was loud, but the mic was so close that his voice was much louder.

The voice's frequencies

  • Fundamental (the pitch): about 85 to 180 Hz for adult men, 165 to 255 Hz for adult women. It carries intonation and loudness but little of the words.
  • Formants: resonances of the throat and mouth that shape vowels. The first sits around 300 to 900 Hz, the second around 900 to 2500 Hz; the first two largely decide which vowel you hear.
  • Consonants and sibilants: bursts and hiss from about 2 to 8 kHz and beyond. Quiet, short, and where most of the difference between words lives.

Sampling at 16 kHz keeps everything up to 8 kHz (half the rate, the Nyquist limit). That covers the fundamental, formants and most of the sibilant energy, which is why most speech recognizers take 16 kHz input and why the app's spectrogram stops at 8 kHz. The telephone band, 300 to 3400 Hz, keeps the vowels and drops much of the consonant detail: you can follow a phone call, but "f" and "s" blur.

Noise floor, speech level, separation

Audio Debugger cuts the signal into short frames (50 ms for a saved sample; the live readout uses its 30-per-second readings of about 43 ms each), takes the RMS of each, and sorts the last 20 seconds of them:

  • Noise floor = the 10th percentile. In normal speech at least a tenth of the time is pauses, so this lands in the gaps and measures the room and the mic's own hiss.
  • Speech level = the 95th percentile: the loud part of your voice.
  • Speech − floor = the separation between them, in dB.

This is a practical stand-in for SNR (signal-to-noise ratio), not the lab measurement. A textbook SNR compares average speech power with average noise power; the 95th percentile sits above the speech average, so separation reads several dB higher than a published SNR for the same recording. Keep that in mind when comparing with papers.

Worked example: a whisper mic at the gym

A one-minute call from a gym, through a close-talking whisper mic, 16 kHz mono, cut into 50 ms frames:

ReadingValueWhat it says
Noise floor (10th percentile)-62 dBFSBetween words the gym barely registers: the mic is close and faces the mouth.
Median frame-50 dBFSHalf the frames are quieter than this. It sits near the floor because a conversation is mostly listening and pauses.

Speech − floor≈ 45 dBGood. Speech is about 180 times the floor in amplitude.

The lesson is distance, not the room. Level falls about 6 dB each time you double the distance from a source, so a mic 2 cm from the mouth instead of 30 cm gains over 20 dB on the voice while the gym, metres away, stays the same. That gap is bought before any processing. A laptop mic on the same treadmill would see the same gym and a much quieter you.

These bands are the app's rule of thumb for this percentile measure, not a published standard. How much noise a recognizer tolerates varies too much by model and by kind of noise to quote one number: other people talking hurts far more than a fan at the same level. Recognizers get worse as separation shrinks, so the useful habit is comparing: the same sentence, two mics or two positions, and keep the bigger number.

What noise reduction trades away

The replay's noise reduction (N) learns the noise from the quietest fifth of the clip and turns down each frequency that does not rise above it. On steady noise it works well and the floor drops. The costs: quiet sounds that look like noise go too (word onsets, final consonants, breathy speech), the remaining noise can warble ("musical noise"), and it cannot remove sounds that change, such as other voices. It also flatters the meter: separation measured after noise reduction is bigger, but the words are not clearer by that much. Judge a mic on its raw separation, then use noise reduction for listening.

3. Voice activity detection

Voice activity detection is the part of a voice app that decides "someone is talking now" and "they have finished". It is like a friend who waits for you to stop talking before they answer.

If the friend answers too fast, they cut you off when you pause to think. If they wait too long, every answer feels slow. And in a noisy room the friend gets confused: is that loud sound you, or the treadmill, or someone across the room?

Two kinds of detector

Threshold (energy) VADs call a frame speech when its level is some margin above the noise floor. They are cheap and predictable, and they know nothing about voices: anything loud enough counts.

Model VADs (classic statistical ones such as WebRTC's, or small neural networks such as Silero) look at the spectrum over time and output a probability that a frame contains speech. They ignore a steady fan much better. They still answer "is there speech?", not "is this my speech?", so a TV or a conversation nearby counts.

The hard cases

  • Breaths are broadband and close to the mic, so energy VADs fire on them and model VADs sometimes do. A close-talking mic makes breaths loud.
  • Background speech is the worst noise there is for a VAD and for a recognizer: it has exactly the shape of the thing being detected.
  • Quiet word edges. Onsets ("f", "th", "h") and trailing consonants are the quietest part of a word, so a detector that waits for loud frames clips them. Systems keep a short pre-roll buffer (a few hundred ms) so the start of the word survives.

Hangover and endpointing

Hangover keeps the detector in the speech state for a short time after the level drops, so the gaps inside and between words do not split the utterance. Endpointing is the larger decision: after how much silence is the turn over and the audio sent? A voice assistant that sends after a 1.5 s pause feels patient but slow; at 0.5 s it answers quickly and cuts people off mid-thought. Long pauses while exercising or thinking push it the wrong way.

Why noise fools it

The threshold has to sit above the noise floor. When the floor rises (the elliptical speeds up, the HVAC kicks in) one of two things happens: the threshold stays put and noise counts as speech, so the endpoint never fires and the app keeps listening; or the threshold adapts upward, and quiet speech (a whisper, the end of a sentence) falls under it and is cut. Separation is the margin the detector has to work with: at 45 dB there is room for a clean threshold; at 10 dB there is not, whatever the algorithm.

Impulsive noise adds false starts: a dropped weight is short and broadband, which an energy detector reads as a syllable. Upstream noise suppression makes the reverse error, covered in the next section.

4. Codecs and the mic path

Sound from a wired or USB mic goes straight into the computer, untouched. Sound from Bluetooth earbuds is squeezed small to fit through the wireless link, a bit like a phone call, and the earbuds also try to scrub out noise before sending it.

Squeezing throws away the high, hissy sounds that tell "s" from "f". Scrubbing sometimes throws away the start of a word because it looked like noise. A person can still understand you; a computer listening for short words often cannot.

Bluetooth: one link, two modes

Listening to music, earbuds use a one-way, high-quality profile (A2DP; Apple devices send AAC). The moment an app opens the earbuds' microphone, the link switches to the two-way hands-free profile (HFP), and both directions drop to a speech codec:

HFP codecSample rateKeeps up toCalls it
CVSD8 kHz≈ 3.4–4 kHzNarrowband, telephone quality; the mandatory fallback
mSBC16 kHz≈ 7–8 kHzWideband speech (HFP 1.6)
LC3-SWB32 kHz≈ 14–16 kHzSuper-wideband (HFP 1.9); needs both ends to support it

Which codec runs is negotiated between the phone or Mac and the earbuds, and a link can fall back to CVSD. That is why Audio Debugger's mic buttons warn that opening a Bluetooth mic switches the headset to call mode: the music you are playing drops to call quality too.

A USB mic sends uncompressed PCM at 44.1 or 48 kHz: no codec, no Bluetooth, and usually no processing beyond the mic's own gain. It is the reference to compare against.

What band limits do to recognition

A recognizer trained on 16 kHz audio expects energy up to 8 kHz. Narrowband audio upsampled to 16 kHz arrives with an empty top half: sibilants and fricatives are gone or folded together, and accuracy drops on exactly the short words where one consonant decides the answer ("six"/"fix", "three"/"free"). The spectrogram shows this directly: on a narrowband path everything above about 4 kHz is black.

What noise suppression does to VAD and ASR

Headset DSP suppresses noise before the codec, and phones and browsers can add their own suppression and automatic gain on top. Suppressors are tuned for continuous conversation: they adapt to what looks steady and turn down what looks like a transient. A short word out of silence looks like a transient, so its onset is turned down until the suppressor decides it is speech. The VAD downstream then sees a clipped word, and the recognizer sees a fragment. Recognizers are mostly trained on raw noisy audio, so aggressive suppression in front of them often fails to help and can make errors worse, while making the floor, and the separation reading, look excellent.

The case study When AirPods Eat Your Words shows this with real recordings: the same words through AirPods arrive as 0.3 s slivers and through a USB mic arrive whole.

The iPhone with AirPods

On the iPhone the path is: the AirPods' mics, their own beamforming and noise suppression, a call-mode voice codec over Bluetooth, iOS's audio session, then the app. Which codec depends on the model: developers have reported recent AirPods using Apple's AAC-ELD at 24 kHz for calls, where older ones use HFP's mSBC or CVSD, and Apple does not document it. Whatever the codec, opening the mic puts the AirPods in call mode, and the iPhone's audio session typically drops to 16 kHz while it lasts.

Audio Debugger lists AirPods' mic last and never opens it by itself, so your music keeps playing in full quality. It asks iOS for .measurement mode, which turns off the system's own voice processing and automatic gain, so what the app shows is what arrived over Bluetooth. It cannot switch off the processing inside the AirPods, which happens before the radio. To see what that path costs, record the same sentence on the iPhone's built-in mic and on the AirPods and compare them in replay: look for an empty top of the spectrogram and softened word starts.
